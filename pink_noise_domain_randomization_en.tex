\subsection{Colored Noise as Observation Domain Randomization}
\label{sec:obs_colored_noise}

\subsubsection{Background: Domain Randomization in Reinforcement Learning}

Deep reinforcement learning (RL) has demonstrated remarkable success in acquiring complex control policies within simulated environments.
However, policies trained purely in simulation often fail to transfer to real physical systems due to the so-called \emph{Sim-to-Real gap}---the inevitable mismatch between the simulator's dynamics, sensor models, and the physical reality~\cite{tobin2017domain,peng2018sim}.

Domain randomization addresses this gap by deliberately introducing variability into simulation parameters during training.
Consider a standard RL formulation where an agent interacts with a Markov Decision Process (MDP) defined by the tuple $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma)$, consisting of the state space $\mathcal{S}$, action space $\mathcal{A}$, transition dynamics $P$, reward function $R$, and discount factor $\gamma$.
In practice, the agent does not observe the true state $\boldsymbol{s}_t \in \mathcal{S}$ but instead receives an observation $\boldsymbol{o}_t = h(\boldsymbol{s}_t)$ through a sensor model $h$.
Domain randomization perturbs both the dynamics $P$ and the observation function $h$ during training, forcing the policy $\pi_\theta(\boldsymbol{a}_t \mid \boldsymbol{o}_t)$ to become robust across a distribution of environment configurations.

Standard approaches randomize dynamics parameters such as body masses, friction coefficients, and joint damping at the start of each episode~\cite{tobin2017domain}.
For observation randomization, independent white noise---typically uniform or Gaussian---is commonly added to corrupt the observation at each step:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{\boldsymbol{o}}_t = \boldsymbol{o}_t + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t, \quad
  \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}, \sigma^2 \boldsymbol{I})
  \label{eq:white_noise}
\end{equation}
where each $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t$ is sampled independently at every time step $t$.

\subsubsection{Limitations of White Noise Observation Randomization}

While conceptually simple, white noise observation randomization suffers from a fundamental modeling limitation.
The noise vector $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:white_noise} is temporally uncorrelated: the samples at time $t$ and $t+1$ are statistically independent.
However, real sensor errors exhibit rich temporal structure~\cite{eberhard2023pink}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Encoder quantization drift:} Motor encoders produce errors that persist over multiple control cycles due to mechanical backlash and hysteresis.
  \item \textbf{Thermal bias:} Analog sensor offsets shift slowly as components heat up during operation.
  \item \textbf{Communication latency:} Jitter in serial communication introduces correlated measurement delays.
\end{itemize}

These phenomena are characterized by \emph{low-frequency-dominant} error spectra---precisely the regime where white noise (which distributes energy uniformly across all frequencies) provides no useful training signal.
A policy trained with only white noise observation corruption learns to filter high-frequency perturbations but remains vulnerable to the persistent, slowly-varying biases encountered on real hardware.

\subsubsection{Colored Noise: Definition and Properties}

Colored noise is a class of stochastic processes whose power spectral density (PSD) follows a power-law relationship with frequency:
\begin{equation}
  S(f) \propto \frac{1}{f^{\beta}}
  \label{eq:psd}
\end{equation}
where $\beta \geq 0$ is the spectral exponent that controls the frequency distribution of energy.
Table~\ref{tab:noise_types} summarizes common colored noise types.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Classification of colored noise by spectral exponent}
  \label{tab:noise_types}
  \begin{tabular}{lcl}
    \toprule
    Noise Type & $\beta$ & Temporal Characteristics \\
    \midrule
    White noise & $0$ & No temporal correlation; flat spectrum \\
    Pink noise  & $1$ & Moderate temporal correlation; $1/f$ spectrum \\
    Brown noise & $2$ & Strong temporal correlation; random walk--like \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Pink noise ($\beta = 1$) occupies a middle ground: it introduces meaningful low-frequency structure---modeling slow bias drift and persistent offsets---while retaining sufficient high-frequency energy to avoid overly smooth, predictable perturbations.
This balance makes it a compelling candidate for modeling real-world sensor noise in domain randomization.

Eberhard~et~al.~\cite{eberhard2023pink} demonstrated that replacing Gaussian action noise with pink noise in policy exploration significantly improves sample efficiency and final performance across a range of continuous control benchmarks.
In this work, we extend this principle from the action space to the observation space, proposing \emph{colored noise observation randomization} as a domain randomization technique for Sim-to-Real transfer.

\subsubsection{Pink Noise Generation via the Timmer--Koenig Algorithm}

We generate pink noise sequences using the spectral synthesis method of Timmer \& Koenig~\cite{timmer1995}, which produces exact $1/f^\beta$ noise through manipulation of Fourier coefficients.

Given a desired sequence length $T$ and dimensionality $d$, the algorithm produces a noise tensor $\boldsymbol{\eta} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times T}$ as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Frequency grid.}
        Compute discrete frequency bins $f_k = k / T$ for $k = 0, 1, \ldots, \lfloor T/2 \rfloor$.
  \item \textbf{Amplitude shaping.}
        Construct scaling factors $s_k = f_k^{-\beta/2}$ that shape the amplitude spectrum.
        To prevent divergence at $f = 0$, define a low-frequency cutoff $f_{\min} = 1/T$ and set $s_k = s_{k_{\min}}$ for all $f_k < f_{\min}$.
  \item \textbf{Random Fourier coefficients.}
        Sample real and imaginary parts of the Fourier coefficients independently:
        $\mathrm{Re}(X_k), \mathrm{Im}(X_k) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, s_k^2)$.
        Enforce Hermitian symmetry constraints: set $\mathrm{Im}(X_0) = 0$ (DC component), and if $T$ is even, set $\mathrm{Im}(X_{T/2}) = 0$ (Nyquist component).
        Multiply the real parts of these constrained components by $\sqrt{2}$ to preserve total energy.
  \item \textbf{Inverse transform and normalization.}
        Apply the Inverse Discrete Fourier Transform (IDFT) to obtain the time-domain signal, then normalize to unit variance:
        \begin{equation}
          \boldsymbol{\eta} = \frac{\mathrm{IDFT}(\boldsymbol{X})}{\sigma_{\mathrm{th}}}, \qquad
          \sigma_{\mathrm{th}} = \frac{2}{T} \sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor T/2 \rfloor} w_k^2}
          \label{eq:normalization}
        \end{equation}
        where $w_k = s_k$ for most bins, with the final bin corrected as $w_{\lfloor T/2 \rfloor} = s_{\lfloor T/2 \rfloor} \cdot (1 + (T \bmod 2))/2$.
\end{enumerate}

The resulting process $\boldsymbol{\eta}$ has unit variance and a PSD proportional to $1/f^\beta$, providing a principled and controllable source of temporally correlated noise.

\subsubsection{Observation Corruption with Colored Noise}

Let $\boldsymbol{o}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ denote the observation vector at time step $t$ in a reinforcement learning environment.
We partition the observation into sensor-derived components $\boldsymbol{o}_t^{\mathrm{(sens)}}$ (e.g., joint positions, velocities, force readings) and non-sensor components $\boldsymbol{o}_t^{\mathrm{(cmd)}}$ (e.g., target commands, previous actions):
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{o}_t = \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{o}_t^{\mathrm{(sens)}} \\ \boldsymbol{o}_t^{\mathrm{(cmd)}} \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

Colored noise is applied selectively to the sensor-derived components:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{\boldsymbol{o}}_t^{\mathrm{(sens)}} = \boldsymbol{o}_t^{\mathrm{(sens)}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{obs}} \, \boldsymbol{\eta}_t
  \label{eq:obs_corruption}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\eta}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{sens}}}$ is drawn from a pink noise process with unit variance, and $\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}$ is a scalar scale parameter controlling the noise magnitude.
Non-sensor components remain uncorrupted, as they originate from the control system or the policy itself and do not suffer from physical measurement error.

The corrupted observation fed to the policy is then:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{\boldsymbol{o}}_t = \begin{bmatrix} \tilde{\boldsymbol{o}}_t^{\mathrm{(sens)}} \\ \boldsymbol{o}_t^{\mathrm{(cmd)}} \end{bmatrix}
  \label{eq:corrupted_obs}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Contrast with action noise.}
While colored action noise~\cite{eberhard2023pink} perturbs the \emph{exploration strategy} of the policy during training (modifying $\boldsymbol{a}_t$ before it reaches the environment), colored observation noise serves a fundamentally different role: it perturbs the \emph{information channel} through which the policy perceives the world.
This distinction is important because the two mechanisms target different sources of the Sim-to-Real gap---actuator imprecision versus sensor imprecision---and their effects on transfer performance may be complementary.

\subsubsection{Efficient Implementation via Noise Buffers}

Generating pink noise through the FFT algorithm incurs $O(T \log T)$ computational cost per sequence.
In large-scale GPU-parallelized RL training with $N$ environments running simultaneously, regenerating noise at every step would be prohibitively expensive.

We adopt a \emph{buffer-based} implementation strategy:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item At initialization (or upon environment reset), pre-generate a noise sequence of length $T$ (typically set to the maximum episode length) for each parallel environment, yielding a buffer $\boldsymbol{H} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d \times T}$.
  \item At each step, advance an index pointer and retrieve the next sample from the buffer in $O(1)$ time.
  \item When an individual environment $i$ is reset (e.g., upon episode termination), regenerate only the buffer slice $\boldsymbol{H}[i, :, :]$, leaving all other environments undisturbed.
\end{enumerate}

This approach amortizes the FFT cost over $T$ steps and ensures that the temporal correlation structure of the noise is preserved across consecutive steps within each episode.

\subsubsection{Relationship to the Domain Randomization Framework}

Colored observation noise can be understood within the broader \emph{Uniform Domain Randomization} (UDR) framework~\cite{tobin2017domain} as a randomization of the sensor model $h$.
Rather than randomizing $h$ through parameter perturbation (e.g., adding a random bias at episode start), colored noise continuously corrupts the observation signal with a statistically structured process, providing a richer and more realistic perturbation than i.i.d.\ noise.

Formally, we define an augmented observation function:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{h}(\boldsymbol{s}_t; \boldsymbol{\eta}_{1:t}) = h(\boldsymbol{s}_t) + \sigma_{\mathrm{obs}} \, \boldsymbol{\eta}_t
  \label{eq:augmented_obs}
\end{equation}
where the noise process $\{\boldsymbol{\eta}_t\}_{t \geq 1}$ introduces path-dependent perturbations: the corruption at time $t$ depends on the entire noise trajectory, not just the current state.
This transforms the standard MDP into a Partially Observable MDP (POMDP) with structured observation noise, providing a more faithful approximation of real-world deployment conditions.
